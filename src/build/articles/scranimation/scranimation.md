### (A brief history of animation in Scratch!)

Scratch is a block-based children’s coding website, made to teach kids how to program their own games. But you can animate with it, too!

To animate in Scratch, you can use either the [vector](https://en.scratch-wiki.info/wiki/Paint_Editor#Vector_Tools) or [bitmap](https://en.scratch-wiki.info/wiki/Paint_Editor#Bitmap_Tools) modes to draw costumes, and make them play sequentially using blocks. Bitmap has better brush control and allows for more stylized graphics, but most Scratchers use vector because it gives them greater control over individual components within the costumes. With vector, it is also possible to make specialized rigs, a vital tool when animating the same characters time and time again.

But way, way, wayyyy back in the ancient times of [Scratch 1.0](https://en.scratch-wiki.info/wiki/Scratch_1.0), only bitmap tools were available in the editor. It was back during those olden days that one of the first prominent Scratch animators, @Astro947, made his debut.

He began to grow in popularity after he released [The Candy Bar](https://scratch.mit.edu/projects/1191712), a short animated skit about a failed chocolate robbery, on July 14th, 2010. He continued to release projects throughout the 2010s, but he is still most well known for [The Candy Bar Saga](https://scratch.mit.edu/studios/5206292/). His persona is a stickman with black outlines and a three-spiked hairstyle, and back then his animation style used Scratch’s built-in bitmap editor, since that was the only editor available in [Scratch 1.4](https://en.scratch-wiki.info/wiki/Scratch_1.4). Some other popular Scratch animators from this era include @scratchu8, @zevo, @SpriteMaster, and @jonzo, who all used Scratch’s bitmap editor to make animations.

But on May 9th, 2013, Scratch was updated to [2.0](https://en.scratch-wiki.info/wiki/Scratch_2.0), which first introduced vector graphics. It was during this time that @WazzoTV began to experiment with his style, transitioning from the classic 1.0 bitmap graphics into a sleeker, crispier vector design. He became extremely popular during 2013 and was the 2nd most followed scratcher during this period, right behind @griffpatch. Some other animators who gained traction during Scratch 2.0 include @-Rocket-, @Hobson-TV, and @ThePancakeMan, who each had their own unique ways of utilizing Scratch’s vector system.

One of Wazzo’s most popular characters was Jarquanzela, a man with a gold chain, green beanie, and dreadlocks who hosted his own cooking show. He first created the character in his project “[How To Make Toast](http://scratch.mit.edu/projects/11560284/)” and continued to animate with him in his series “Cooking with Jarquanzela.” His own persona, who he called Wazzo (pronounced “Wazzoo”), had a red shirt, brown hair, rectangular torso, and stick limbs, along with a unique square-shaped mouth. In 2019, he would direct [The Scratch Saga](https://youtu.be/DCH4vO_Lq4Q) with Astro947, which would inspire future Scratchers to create their own series.

One of these scratchers was @huntedskelly, who started his series [The Unfamous](https://scratch.mit.edu/projects/334931082) soon after the release of [Scratch 3.0](https://en.scratch-wiki.info/wiki/Scratch_3.0). He collaborated with over 100 Scratchers, including animators like @7sofa, @-BoyMcBoy-, @sharkyshar, and @GoldenEagleStudios. The full movie is over 1.4 hours long, and I’d personally consider it to be one of the most ambitious animated projects ever made on Scratch.

Nowadays, you can find a variety of different animators on the platform, each with a unique style. As time has moved on, animators continue to push the platform to its logical extremes. Many animators like @Giolaboman and @ArcticFoxieAnimates use a mixture of external softwares and the native Scratch engine to create higher quality animations than what would normally be achievable. Others like @YeetachuAnimation and @LazyEyeTV used the platform to create their own indie animated series: [The Yeetachuniverse Pilot](https://scratch.mit.edu/projects/1117024987) (currently with two episodes) and [The Malware Arc](https://scratch.mit.edu/studios/34480852/) respectively. Some animators even use their works on Scratch in their official portfolios and animation reels, like @FUZZIE-WEASEL and @TailsKirbyYoshi205.

There are a variety of different methods to approaching Scratch animation. You can use the [CattyCodes Method](https://youtu.be/siK1rlb6Ycw) with TurboWarp, [Scratch Addons](https://scratchaddons.com) for better tools, [BrickPlex](https://scratch.mit.edu/users/BrickPlex)’s [WarpSpeed](https://warpspeed0.github.io/WarpSpeed/) for an experience designed with animators in mind, or simply vanilla scratch. (I have a tutorial for using vanilla Scratch with animations [here](https://youtu.be/HvebgRyU95w).)

So, with all that said, have fun Scranimating!
